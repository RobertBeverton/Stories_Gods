## Part One. Cones in the Playground

On Monday morning, the crack in the playground had a fence around it.

It wasn't a very good fence. It was made of six orange traffic cones and a roll of red-and-white tape that said CAUTION. But it stopped most people from falling in, which was the main thing. A thin curl of steam rose from the crack. The tarmac around it was warm and soft, and a bit sticky, like toffee.

Sitting on the edge of the cones, with her bare feet dangling over the crack, was Miss Pele.

She had a long red cloak, and black hair streaked with orange that moved slowly, as if it were being blown by a breeze that nobody else could feel. Her badge said Miss Pele, Home Economics and Hot Water. Wherever she walked, her footprints glowed, and then faded, and left behind a faint smell of toast.

She did not look happy.

"Don't go near her," whispered Ollie, as we filed past. "She looks like she might go off."

"She looks sad," said Felix.

"She looks cross," said Alex.

"She looks both," said Felix.

Elfine and Marion were already waiting by the gate, with Willow tucked under Marion's arm. Willow was not purring. She had her head turned away from the crack and her whiskers laid back. Even the nine-year-old Reading Cat of Year One thought this was too hot.

"I've been reading about her all weekend," whispered Elfine. She pushed her glasses up her nose. "I think this is going to be a tricky one."

## Part Two. Elfine Has a Fact

We sat on the far side of the playground, as far from the crack as we could get. Elfine opened her notebook and read from her neatest page.

"Pele is the goddess of fire and volcanoes," she said. "She comes from Hawai'i, the islands in the middle of the Pacific Ocean. She is very powerful, and very old. And even now, people in Hawai'i still honour her and tell her stories with great respect. She lives in the crater of a mountain called Kilauea. Sometimes that mountain erupts, and glowing rivers of lava flow down to the sea."

"Hawai'i," said Dot slowly. "That's where Mister Maui comes from."

"The islands are the ones he fished up with his hook," said Alex.

"And Pele is the one who lives in them," said Elfine. "She's their fire."

"But why is she in our playground?" said Marion.

"I think she's running away," said Elfine. "In the old story, Pele used to live far away, in a place called Tahiti. She had lots of brothers and sisters. And one of them was Namaka, the goddess of the sea."

"Her sister is the sea?" said Ollie.

"And they didn't get along," said Elfine. "They quarrelled and quarrelled. So Pele sailed away in a canoe with her family. She was looking for a new home, where she could light a fire and keep it burning. And she dug a fire pit on one island. But her sister Namaka followed her in a great rolling wave, and put it out. So Pele moved to the next island, and the next. Each time she dug a pit, Namaka sent the sea after her. Pele was hot, and Namaka was wet, and neither one would give in."

"That's silly," said Dot.

"It's very silly," said Elfine. "Until at last, on the biggest island, Pele climbed right up to the top of a mountain, so high that the sea could never reach her. And she's lived there ever since."

"So why's she here?" said Felix.

"I don't know," said Elfine. "But I'm guessing Namaka is on her way."

## Part Three. The Floor Is Lava

It was Tuesday when everything went wrong.

At break time, the Reception class were playing their favourite game. It's called The Floor Is Lava, and it goes like this. You pretend the ground is a river of burning lava, and you have to climb onto the nearest high thing before you get burnt.

"The floor is LAVA!" shrieked Dot, and the whole of Reception scrambled up the climbing frame.

It's a fun game. Right up until the moment when it isn't a game.

Miss Pele jerked her head up. Her eyes flashed red. "Lava?" she said, in a dangerous voice. "Who said lava?"

"We're only playing," said Dot, from the top of the slide.

"Everyone always says lava," said Miss Pele. Her hair rose up around her head like flames. "Everyone's always saying calm down, don't be so hot-headed, don't be so fiery. And I try. I really do try. But then it all comes bubbling up, and I can't help it, and..."

The crack in the playground split wider with a bang. A bright orange river of glowing lava came pouring out and spread across the tarmac in every direction.

It was real lava. It oozed. It bubbled. It lit up the whole playground in a flickering orange glow.

"Everybody up!" shouted Mister Harris, blowing his whistle. "Climbing frame! Slide! Anything high!"

Children scrambled onto the benches, the bike shelter and the roofs of the sheds. Reception clung to the top of the climbing frame. And the lava crept closer and closer, until the frame was surrounded on every side.

## Part Four. Marion's Monkey Bars

"They can't get down!" shrieked Maya.

"They'll be stuck up there," said Kenji.

The lava rolled round and round the climbing frame in a glowing ring. The wooden legs started to smoke. Dot and the other Reception children huddled together on the top, crying and holding hands.

"I'll get them," said Marion.

She was already running. Marion has the best climbing legs in school, and she knew exactly what to do. She leaped onto the nearest bench, then onto the roof of the bike shelter, then along the top of the fence, three steps ahead of the lava. At the end of the fence, she jumped. She caught the bottom bar of the monkey bars with both hands, and swung.

"What are you doing?" yelled Elfine.

"The monkey bars go right across!" yelled Marion. "They go all the way to the frame! It's a bridge!"

She swung from bar to bar, hand over hand, her feet dangling above the glowing river. Alex and Felix jumped up beside her, one on each side, hand over hand, and Kenji, Ollie and Maya followed. They made a line of swinging children across the playground, with their toes inches above the heat.

"Come on, Dot!" yelled Felix.

"It's too far!" cried Dot.

"You can do it!" yelled Alex. "One bar at a time!"

Dot wiped her nose. She took a deep, shaky breath. "I'm small," she called, "but I've got strong arms!"

And one by one, hand over hand, with Marion and the twins holding on to her, the Reception children crossed the burning playground, until every last one was safe.

## Part Five. The Lollipop Lady

That was when a whistle blew.

It wasn't Mister Harris. It was a thin, clear, cool whistle, like the sound a wave makes, sliding up the sand.

A woman was standing in the middle of the road outside the school gates, holding up a big round STOP sign. It was shaped like a wave. She wore a long, shimmering sea-green coat, covered in tiny shells, and her hair rippled over her shoulders like water. Her badge said Miss Namaka, Lollipop Lady.

She did not look hurried. She did not look cross. She looked completely calm, like a pond with no wind on it.

Behind her, a line of cars had stopped dead. The drivers were all staring.

"Hello, Pele," said Namaka, in a voice like a rolling tide.

"Go away," said Miss Pele.

"I've been looking for you for three weeks," said Namaka. She walked slowly through the school gates. The wet tarmac hissed wherever her feet touched it. "You left in the middle of the night. You didn't even leave a note."

"I didn't want to be found," said Pele.

"I know," said Namaka. "That's the problem. You never do."

The lava fizzed. The air was getting hotter. Pele stood up, and the ground cracked under her feet. Namaka raised her STOP sign, and a swirling curtain of mist came rolling in off the road.

"She's going to put out the fire," whispered Elfine.

"She's going to flood the school," said Marion.

"They're both going to do something terrible," said Kenji. "I can feel it."

## Part Six. Alex and Felix Step In

It was Alex and Felix who noticed the thing that everyone else had missed.

Pele and Namaka were shouting at each other, but they weren't saying what they really meant. Pele was shouting about being chased. Namaka was shouting about being left. Neither of them was listening, and both of them were crying.

Alex took Felix's hand, and Felix took Alex's.

They walked through the glowing playground together, right between the two goddesses.

"Excuse me," said Alex, in his clear, careful voice.

"Excuse me," said Felix, in his gentle one.

Pele looked down. Her eyes were red from crying. A tear rolled down her face and sizzled when it hit the ground. Namaka looked down too. Her sea-green eyes were shining.

"We're twins," said Alex.

"We know about fighting," said Felix.

"We fight about all sorts of silly things," said Alex. "Who gets the red cup. Who started it. Who's the tallest."

"I'm the tallest," said Felix.

"No, I am," said Alex.

"We have a rule," said Felix. "When we have a fight that's too big to fix, we count to three. And then we both say sorry. At the same time. And then we have a hug."

"Even if it wasn't our fault?" said Namaka, slowly.

"Especially if we're not sure whose fault it was," said Alex.

"Because then it doesn't matter," said Felix. "You just want to be friends again more than you want to win."

## Part Seven. The Canoe

Pele sat down on the edge of the crack, with her chin in her hands.

"It was the canoe," she said, in a small voice.

"The canoe?" said Felix, and he sat down next to her.

"Namaka had a beautiful canoe," said Pele. "She painted it herself. It had a long curved prow and a little carved bird on the front. And I borrowed it, without asking, to go and look at a volcano. And I got too close, and..." She sniffed. "I burnt it."

"The whole thing?" said Alex, sitting down beside Namaka.

"Right down to the water," said Namaka, in a wobbly voice. "It was my favourite thing in the world."

"And I never said sorry," whispered Pele. "I was too embarrassed. So I ran away. And then you chased me, and I got cross, and the more you chased, the more I ran. And now it's been a hundred years."

"A hundred and four," said Namaka.

"I'm sorry," said Pele. "I should have said it."

"I'm sorry too," said Namaka. "I should have stopped chasing and just asked why."

There was a long pause. The lava gave a gentle bloop. The mist swirled. Neither goddess moved.

"Say it together," said Felix.

"On three," said Alex.

## Part Eight. Willow's Moment

Pele and Namaka looked at each other. Then at Alex and Felix. Then at the ground.

"I can't," said Pele. "I'm too hot. If I hug her, I'll burn her. And she's so wet, she'll put me out."

"That's silly," said Marion, from the monkey bars. "You're sisters. You'll work it out."

"We'll work it out," said Elfine, "because Willow's going to help."

Everyone looked at Willow. She was sitting on the climbing frame bench with her paws tucked neatly under her chest, looking at the goddesses with half-closed golden eyes.

Elfine whispered in Marion's ear. Marion nodded. She picked Willow up gently and carried her across the playground, past the glowing lava, past the swirling mist, and held her out to Pele.

"She's a Reading Cat," said Marion. "She's very good at calming people down."

Pele hesitated. Then, very carefully, she took Willow in her arms.

Willow looked up at her. She blinked, slowly. Then she started to purr.

It was the biggest purr we had ever heard. It rolled out of her chest like a motor, deep and slow and warm. And as it did, the orange glow in Pele's hair began to fade. Her shoulders loosened. The heat around her dropped, a little at a time, until her cloak stopped smoking and her feet stopped glowing.

"Oh," said Pele, softly. "Oh, that helps."

"She does that," said Marion proudly.

"She tolerates us," said Elfine. "But sometimes she makes an exception."

## Part Nine. Sorry on Three

Pele held out her hand to Namaka. It was warm, but not burning. Namaka took it. Her hand was cool, but not freezing.

"On three," said Alex.

"One," said Felix.

"Two," said Alex.

"Three," said both twins.

"Sorry," said Pele and Namaka, at exactly the same time.

And they threw their arms round each other. It was a very big hug. There was a sizzle, and a hiss, and a great billow of white steam that rolled across the playground like a cloud. We couldn't see anything for a moment. We heard laughing, and sniffing, and somebody saying "I missed you so much", and somebody else saying "me too".

When the steam cleared, the two goddesses were standing with their arms round each other's shoulders. Namaka's sea-green hair was tangled up with Pele's red-gold curls. Their cheeks were wet.

"We did it," said Dot, who was sitting on Mister Harris's shoulders. "They said sorry!"

"We always knew you could," said Felix.

"All you needed was a little help," said Alex.

## Part Ten. How Islands Are Made

But the lava was still creeping across the tarmac.

"Oh dear," said Pele, wiping her eyes. "I forgot about that."

"Don't worry," said Namaka. She held up her STOP sign. The wave on it began to glow. "Shall we?"

"Together?" said Pele.

"Together," said Namaka.

Pele held out her hands, and the lava rolled gently towards the edge of the playground. Namaka raised hers, and a soft sparkling spray of seawater drifted over the glowing river. Where the water met the fire, there was a *hiss*, and a puff of steam. And then the lava went dark and still.

It had turned to black rock. Smooth and shiny, like glass. And along the edge, where the waves of spray had settled, a pile of fine, silky black sand had been left behind, like a tiny beach.

"It's an island!" whispered Elfine.

"It's a new bit of land," said Alex slowly. "Look. Fire made it, and water shaped it. They had to work together. Neither one could do it alone."

"That's how islands get made," said Felix.

"That's exactly how," said Pele, with a wobbly smile. "Fire pushes it up out of the ground. And the sea carves it into beaches. We were never meant to be enemies. We were meant to be partners. It just took us a hundred and four years to remember."

All around the playground, the black rock was cooling into smooth, shining patches. In the middle, where the crack had been, there was a round black stone, warm as a hot-water bottle, smooth enough to sit on.

"That's the best seat in the school," said Kenji.

"It'll stay warm all winter," said Pele. "I'll make sure of it."

## Part Eleven. What Pele Said

At the end of the day, Pele sat on the new warm stone and told us something.

She had a cup of cocoa in her hands, which Missus Tibbs had made, and Willow was curled up in her lap, still purring. Namaka sat beside her, with her feet in the school pond, which was suddenly bigger and bluer than it had ever been.

"I'm very, very hot-tempered," said Pele. "Everyone knows it. I flare up quickly, and I say things I don't mean. And I always thought that made me a bad goddess."

"It doesn't," said Felix.

"Having big feelings isn't bad," said Alex.

"I know that now," said Pele. "It's what you do with them that counts. When I'm angry, I can burn things down. Or I can stop and breathe and ask for help. I didn't know I was allowed to ask."

"Everybody is allowed to ask," said Dot.

"And being cross doesn't mean you're not loved," said Namaka, squeezing her sister's hand.

"The bravest thing anyone can do is say sorry first," said Pele. She looked at the twins. "And you two knew that all along. You didn't need magic. You just needed each other."

Marion nudged Elfine. "We should try the counting thing sometimes," she whispered.

"We don't fight," said Elfine.

"We fight about the bathroom," said Marion.

"Fair point," said Elfine.

## Part Twelve. The Ending, with a Hook

Later that week, as the November evenings drew in, something very odd happened.

It was Friday, nearly home time. Pele and Namaka were sitting on the warm stone, comparing shells and rocks and telling each other stories. Mister Hadad was leaning against the cabin with his arms folded, looking pleased with himself. And Alex and Felix, who had a few minutes before their nana arrived, were sitting on the steps by the school door, watching the sunset.

That was when Dot, who notices things, tugged on Felix's sleeve.

"Look," she said. "On the windowsill."

We looked. On the windowsill of every classroom, as far as we could see, there was a small clay lamp. They were round and shallow, like tiny saucers, and each one had a little flame dancing in the middle. There were hundreds of them. They twinkled all the way along the building.

"Who put those there?" said Maya, flipping open her notebook.

"I didn't," said Mister Harris. He looked baffled.

"It's Diwali soon," whispered Elfine. "It's the festival of lights. It's in just over a week."

On the steps below us, in the damp earth by the door, was a single line of tiny footprints. They were perfectly formed, and they were shining like gold, and they led from the school gate to the front door.

A single pink lotus flower lay on the doorstep.

"Lotus," said Elfine, very quietly. "Gold footprints. Lamps." She looked up at the doorway. "I know who that is."

Pele and Namaka stood up and looked at each other. Hadad took off his helmet. Even Willow lifted her head and sniffed.

A voice, soft as silk, drifted out of the school hallway. "Is anyone in? I've come to bring good luck."

Everyone looked at Alex and Felix. They looked at each other, with the golden light flickering in their eyes. They said, at exactly the same time, "Monday."

The End.
