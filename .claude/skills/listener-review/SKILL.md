# Listener review

Read the outline or draft once for each persona below and note only what that persona would genuinely notice. Then cross-review the notes: where two personas flag the same passage, propose one edit that serves both. Not everything needs fixing; separate real problems from minor, net-neutral notes.

## Personas

**Alex, aged 5.** Is he in it as much as his brother? Does he speak, decide, act and get the funny line? Does he have a moment that is his alone, and a moment shared? Count his lines and actions.

**Felix, aged 5.** The same questions. Compare the two counts side by side. A gap of more than a few lines or any lopsided big moment (one saves the day, the other watches) is a real problem. Check who goes first, and that they say the key thing together.

**A five-year-old listener.** Is there a part that is too frightening, confusing or long without a laugh? Is there a word or idea too hard even with context? Does anything feel unfair or mean without being put right? Is there a moment to laugh at least once a part? Are Willow and Dot doing something fun?

**The parent reading aloud.** Can every sentence be said in one breath? Are there tongue-twisters, confusing name clusters, or runs of dialogue where it is hard to tell who is speaking? Is the length right (about 25 to 30 minutes; up to about 35 if the story earns it)? Could a parent perform it without the audio: is it always clear which twin is acting, and are animal sounds narrated rather than spelled as speech? Does the ending land warmly, even with a hook? Is there anything a six-year-old could copy and get hurt, or a grown-up who is left cruelly dismissed or trapped? Is there any part that would make a tired parent wince?

**A sensitivity reader for the tradition.** Would someone from this culture or faith recognise their god and myth with respect? Are names and terms right? Is anything played for a laugh that should not be? Does anything flatten a living religion into a cartoon? Is the dark part of the myth handled gently, and not removed in a way that changes its meaning?

**A five-year-old listener, again: the gap.** Is there a moment where the listener knows something the characters don't (Willow sees the lady, Mister Harris says "Nobody there")? Those were the big laughs in story 39. Is there one, and is it clear?

**An editor.** Is the shape sound (the 17-part recipe is a rough guide)? Does the middle sag? Tag each part's tension type: are any two the same back to back? Is the biggest beat at or near the climax, not spent early? Is there a quiet beat after the scariest moment, and a tender part before the hook? Is everything the plot needs late (a skill, an object, a rule) planted early, and does a new task fail before it works? Does each part end with a reason to turn the page? Are there repeated phrases or set-pieces from the last few stories? Is the affirmation earned by the plot and short enough to say back? Is the hook a real mystery?

## The review loop

The user's standing workflow for a draft: persona review, read and assess, fix; persona review again on the revised text, read and assess, fix; then the adversarial pass below, read and assess, fix. Re-read the whole text before each round: fixes create new slips (a merged line that doubles a speech tag, a new sentence that contradicts an old one). A round that finds nothing real is a fine result; say so rather than inventing changes.

## Adversarial pass

Try to break the story rather than enjoy it. Look for:
- **Plot holes:** why doesn't the hero just do the obvious thing? Could a staff god (nearly forty of them) solve this in one line? Does the magic follow its own stated rule every time?
- **Unshown claims:** anything the god or a child says happened ("Willow sat with me this morning") that never did on the page.
- **Canon slips:** small facts from earlier stories about the regulars (Willow permits a hug for exactly two seconds; Mister Harris is afraid of the dark; Mr Thorley must not be startled).
- **Copy risks:** every physical stunt a six-year-old could repeat at home, however small (walking backwards on mats on the stairs, opening the door to a knock in the dark without a grown-up).
- **Dangling plants:** small set-ups never used (Willow's three words, a half-written word in Maya's notebook).
- **The hostile reader from the tradition:** the line a devout or culturally close reader would wince at, even if harmless to a child (a saint making a rhyme about a mouse's bottom).

## Output

1. A short verdict per persona (one or two lines).
2. The twin count: lines spoken and key actions for Alex and Felix.
3. The cross-reviewed fix list, most important first, with the passage to change.
4. Minor notes, if any.
