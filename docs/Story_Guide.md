# The Staff Room Gods: Story Guide

How to write a story in this series. It is written so that anyone (a parent, a Claude Code session, a friend writing for their own children) can pick it up cold. `Story_Bible.md` holds the specifics for this family's cast; this guide holds the method.

## The idea

A god or legend from a different world mythology turns up as a new member of school staff. The children work out who they are, tell the real myth, and help the god and the school through a problem that echoes the myth. Each story is about 4,000 words (a 25 to 30 minute read-aloud), told by a group of classmates saying "we", and ends on a hook for the next god.

## The recipe: about 16 to 18 short parts (a rough guide)

Each part is a short chapter of about 200 to 300 words with a plain heading, for example `## Part Three. The Sixteen and the One`. Short chapters keep young listeners with you and give natural stopping points.

| Part | What happens |
|---|---|
| 1. Something odd arrives | A strange sign of the god turns up on Monday morning: a golden pond, a giant feather, a smell of woodsmoke. Nobody knows why. |
| 2. The new staff member | A new teacher or helper appears with a badge showing a school job ("Mrs Oshun, Pond and Puddings"). The job hints at who the god really is. |
| 3. The explainer works it out | The bookish friend gathers evidence ("Evidence number one: a mirror") and says who the god is, where they are from, and what they are famous for. |
| 4. The old myth | The explainer tells one real, child-friendly myth from the tradition. This is the engine of the story. |
| 5. The school gets a problem | Something at school echoes the myth: a committee leaves someone out, a tower leans, a fog comes down. |
| 6 to 8. Things go wrong | The problem grows. Grown-ups argue or panic. Real peril builds, with the children on the edge of it. |
| 9. The children see what is needed | The twins realise the myth holds the answer and notice what the adults missed. |
| 10. They ask the god for help | A brave, kind request, usually with a sorry or a please. The children go first, not the adults. |
| 11 to 13. The rescue | The god shows their true shape. The pet or a small friend does something heroic. The twins do the key action together. |
| 14. The grown-ups learn | The adults take the lesson. Nobody is shamed. |
| 15. Things are put right | The school ends up better than before: a new playground, a new tree, a new rule. |
| 16. The god's words | At sunset the god says something wise in plain words. The affirmation lands here. |
| 17. The hook | A strange sign of the next god. The twins say "Monday" at exactly the same time. "The End." |

Parts 6 to 8 and 11 to 13 can run longer, because that is where the action is. The series has used 7 to 24 sections (the short early stories at the low end, the later ones 12 to 21 parts); 16 to 18 is the sweet spot, but the recipe is a guide and not a rule. Aim for at least about 4,000 words; a story that deserves the room can run to about 6,000.

## Story shapes: vary the recipe

By story 38 the recipe above had been used for nearly every story, and the part headings had started to give it away ("Maya's Evidence" at Part Three, "What She Told Us at Sunset", "The Ending, with a Hook"). Keep the recipe for about half the stories. For the rest, pick a variant:

- **A late-reveal mystery.** Maya's evidence builds slowly and the god's identity is only clear near the climax, so the listeners guess along.
- **One strong visual gag and one feeling, with no villain.** Story 38 (Janus) is the model: a school gone strange in a way you can see (every door leads somewhere wrong), a god torn between two feelings, and a small concrete job for the twins (turning the keys).
- **One twin's point of view.** A single story told closely from Alex's or Felix's side. The narrating twin gets the inner thoughts, so the other twin must win on actions: the clever idea or the decisive move. Pair it with a later story told from the other twin's side.
- **Willow's story (once only, done in story 39).** Told in the third person from Willow's point of view. Do not repeat it. What made it work, and carries over to other shapes: it ran on action rather than speeches; every attempt Willow made came from a real habit the family described; each attempt failed in a new way (the wrong guess, the right door with the wrong meaning, a distraction, a frozen tap) before the fifth worked; and the listeners could see what the characters could not (see the next shape).
- **The listener knows more than the characters.** Let the children hear or see something the cast cannot: in story 39, only Willow (and so the listeners) can see the lady on the step, while Mister Harris opens the door and says "Nobody there." The twins laughed all the way through. If you want them to shout along, pantomime style ("She's outside!"), the gap alone is not enough: repeat one prompt line three times, each a little more ridiculous, and leave a beat after it.
- **A return story.** A god from an earlier story comes back with a new problem. Cheaper than a new god and it deepens the world (Janus is the obvious first candidate; see the tracker).

Whatever the shape, give parts headings that intrigue rather than label the beat.

## Voice and style

The feel is Pamela Butchart's "Wigglesbottom Primary": funny, breathless, and told by the children. Capture the spirit; never copy her text.

- **A child "we" narrator.** A group of classmates tells it ("We all stood round it in a ring"). They notice everything and understand about half of it.
- **Deadpan kid logic.** Strange things are reported flatly. "Nobody had put it there. But somebody had to have, because it had definitely not been there on Friday."
- **"That's when" pacing.** Use it three or four times per story to turn a quiet moment into a surprise.
- **Short sentences, short paragraphs, lots of dialogue.** Two to four sentences per paragraph. Easy to read aloud and easy to voice.
- **Reading level above their age.** The children are in Year 1, but the language is pitched at Year 2 to 3. A few big words are fine; context carries them. Put in two or three stretch words per story on purpose ("wretchedly", "extraordinary"), each explained by what is around it. Stories 1 to 38 average Flesch reading ease about 92, which is very easy, so there is room.
- **Vary the rhythm.** Sentences average about 8.7 words in every story (7.4 to 9.9). Keep them short, but let the narrator have an occasional long, rolling sentence that builds ("as wide as a wardrobe, then as wide as a bookcase, then as wide as a cat"). The stories are heard through a narrator voice, so rhythm matters.
- **Vary the big words.** Huge, enormous and massive appear about 4 times a story (143 in stories 1 to 38; story 23 has 13). Find a fresher picture instead ("as big as the dinner hall").
- **Specific, funny details.** A lunchbox from 1987. A cat sneezing at the moment of heroism. A sandwich that was not his.
- **Real peril, always resolved.** Something genuinely frightening happens, the children are scared and brave at once, and nobody is truly hurt.
- **Kindness beats force.** The answer is almost never a fight. It is asking, sharing, saying sorry, listening, or being gentle.
- **A happy ending.** Always. Stories end warm.

## Fairness between siblings

If there is more than one child hero, they must share the spotlight evenly, or one will notice. Check as you write:

- Each child speaks about the same amount.
- They split the big moments (one holds one end of the feather and the other holds the other, one swings one club and the other swings the second).
- They say important things together, in the same breath, as often as separately.
- Neither is "the brave one" across a whole story. Swap roles from story to story.
- One twin can start a thing, but both finish it. Never have one save the day while the other watches.
- In a story told from one twin's point of view, count actions, not lines: the other twin must have a key decision or move of their own.

The real twins listen differently, which is worth knowing: in November 2026 Alex asked for story 38 (Janus) morning and evening, every day, and called it "very funny"; Felix prefers a new story each day and has no favourite yet. Return stories serve one; a steady flow of new gods serves the other.

## Tension, stakes and peril

A story needs real tension that is varied, builds, and is always resolved kindly. These rules came out of the second review pass over the early stories (and from the craft guide of the sister series, Isles of Yum).

- **Name each beat's type.** Mystery or cliffhanger, problem to solve, physical jeopardy, or social and emotional tension. Do not run the same type twice back to back. If a beat repeats the one before it, change its type or shrink it to a texture moment (a joke, a smell, one line).
- **Build to the biggest beat.** The scariest or most affecting moment belongs at or near the climax. If it happens in the middle and then cools off, the story peaks too soon.
- **Physical jeopardy, calibrated.** Cause it by chance or the environment, never malice. Resolve it within about a page. The children's teamwork solves it, with adults present but not the ones who fix it (they can try and fail first). End in relief or a laugh. Give the fright one quiet "catching your breath" beat before the plot speeds up again.
- **The obstacle ladder.** Three or four obstacles, each a different flavour of tension and a different virtue or skill: kindness or asking, a practised skill, nerve, pure teamwork. Never two in a row solved the same way.
- **The learning beat.** If the children have to do something they have never done, it does not work first time. The first try fails (funny but tense), the second wobbles, the third works because one of them notices the fix. Give each twin a different part of the fix.
- **Light and shade.** At least two comic parts, a quiet or tender part before the climax, a quiet part before the hook, and no run of three parts at the same intensity.
- **A taker, never a harmer.** The school problem comes from a mishap, a mistake, or a want taken too far, not from a villain. There is a reason a child can say back, and the fix is a conversation and a small concrete job.
- **Show the dark episode's fear and relief on the page.** If the myth's engine is dark (poison, a flood, fire), let the children be scared where we can see it, then let the relief be just as clear.

## Plant and pay off

- Every punchline, twist or late fix needs an earlier light seed. At outline stage, list every plant with the part where it pays off, and plant by about Part Three.
- Show it, do not just list it. If the evidence list says he lifted a piano, let us see him lift the piano.
- Plant the school problem early (an announcement, a delivery, a deadline) so that when it arrives it is a payoff and not a surprise halfway through.
- A refrain helps: one short, kid-sayable phrase that is asked first by someone with authority, comes back with the children's own spin, and lands last with a changed meaning.
- If the myth's mechanism is magical (a fog that spreads, a cloud that seems heavy), give it one child-sayable rule early and have a child use it aloud, so it reads as earned and not as mood.

## Common problems to catch before filing

These came up again and again when all 38 stories were reviewed. Check them at outline and again at draft.

- **No year groups or ages** in the narration ("Year 2", "aged five"). Say "the school", "the class", "the big kids", "the little ones". "Reception" is fine.
- **One main affirmation, two at most.** Enacted, not recited. Never several in a row, and the god's explaining speech is only a few lines, with a child saying it back in their own words.
- **The ritual ending, every time.** The hook for the next god, then both twins say "Monday" at exactly the same time (not another shared word, and not an adult), then "The End."
- **Nothing a six-year-old could copy and get hurt.** No child kneeling at a smoking door, no adult lighting a match near children without a plain consequence, and a named adult in charge of any group left behind.
- **Grown-ups are kind.** Nobody is left trapped, dismissed, or made the butt of the joke (the dinner lady's soup, the boy in the chair). Mistakes get a sorry and a fix.
- **Willow never speaks** (no meow written as dialogue) and does one brave thing.
- **No clashing names or real brands.** No character named Felix or Alex apart from the twins, and no real book, brand or character named in the text.
- **Keep the tics down.** "Looked at each other", "underlined it three times", "at exactly the same time" outside the ritual, and "That's when" no more than four times. (A count of stories 1 to 38 found "at exactly the same time" in 36 stories, but 33 of those were the Monday ritual itself, which stays. Only story 1 went over four "That's when"s.)
- **Grown-ups can be wise, not only panicky.** Mister Harris flapping is a good joke, but rotate it. His quiet confession in story 38 shows how strong he is when he is not panicking. Rotate the supporting cast too.
- **Willow does her brave thing as a real cat would.** Willow is the twins' real Bengal. Her best moments come from true cat habits (in story 38 she sits in the closing doorway, refusing to be in or out). Ask the family what Willow really does, and build from that.
- **Keep the crowd small.** A roll call of more than about six names at once is hard to follow read aloud.
- **Respect the cast timeline.** Do not use a character before their first story (Dot first appears in story 9) and do not name someone a later story introduces as new. Check the next story's actual opening before writing a hook.
- **Give the quiet parts texture the first time.** Thin "nothing is happening yet" parts are why the early stories came out at 1,000 words.
- **Say so when the story invents.** Where it departs from a living tradition's telling, let Maya say it lightly, and do not state anything about the tradition you are not sure of.
- **Maya is a child, not an encyclopedia.** She can remember half a myth, muddle the order or get a name wrong ("Or was it the cow who wouldn't eat?"); the god can set it straight kindly later. That is funnier and truer than a perfect lecture.
- **Keep the twins' parents out.** The stories are for the boys. Nana can cameo; Mum and Dad stay out, even offstage.
- **Could a staff god solve this in one line?** With nearly forty gods on the staff, check who could fix the problem trivially, and give them a reason they can't: in story 39, Mrs Bast (who understands every cat) was away on a course about mice, and Missus Mahuika's boiler "won't light; it's waiting for something".
- **Pay off the small plants too.** If Part One says Willow knows three words, use all three. If Maya nearly writes the answer, show the page later.
- **The god's speech only claims what we saw.** If the god thanks someone for something ("Willow sat with me this morning"), check it happened on the page.
- **Check the copy risk of every physical stunt**, including the small ones: a child walking backwards down a mat on the stairs, children opening the door to a knock in the dark without a grown-up's nod. Give the stunt to the animal, or add a banister and an adult.
- **Check canon details of the regulars.** Small facts stick: Willow permits a hug "for exactly two seconds" (story 38), so she can't "never" be held.

## Affirmations

The stories teach self-belief without lecturing.

- One main affirmation per story, two at most. Put it in the god's mouth, late in the story, after the action.
- Keep them short and concrete: one or two sentences a child could say back.
- Tie each to what just happened, so the plot earns it.
- No notes page, no quiz, no moral box afterwards. Do not ask the children questions at the end.
- Let a child echo it in their own words.

Examples used so far: "Everybody needs a chair." "A sweet heart is not a soft one." "Being lost is not something wrong with you. It is only a place you happen to be standing for a while." "A thank you is a seed." "The ground holds you up." "Hard things take as long as they take." "Ask for what you need. You would be amazed how often the answer is yes." Rotate themes: sharing, asking for help, saying sorry, including everyone, patience, being brave while scared, being gentle and strong at once, trusting that someone always comes.

## The hook ending

The last scene shows a strange sign of the next god, never naming them: a giant feather and a voice, a crack in the sky, a llama in a woven hat. The twins then say "Monday" at exactly the same time. The next story must pick up that hook, unless the person asking chooses another god.

The "Monday" ritual stays in every story: the children may well be saying it along with the narrator. What has gone stale is the sentence that leads into it. Stories 34 to 38 all end almost word for word "Alex and Felix looked at each other... They said, at exactly the same time, 'Monday.' The End." Change the lead-in each time (what they are holding, where they are standing, what they have just heard), and vary the hook's form: a sign of the next figure, a question left open, or one quiet strange line.

## Choosing the god

**Pick the feeling first, then the god.** Do not repeat the lesson of any of the last five stories, and check the "Feeling" and "Main attribute" columns in `God_Tracker.md` (six tricksters and several fire figures are already covered; "asking for help" has come up in stories 1, 30 and 37). The best feelings come from the twins' own lives: story 38 worked because the god's problem (sad and glad at once) was the twins' own first day at school, remembered on the page. Look for things they have really been through: a wobbly tooth, swimming lessons, a sleepover, Nana going home.

Then pick a different tradition each time. A good choice has three things:

1. A **memorable look** (a feather, a snake's tail, a golden pond).
2. A **story with a clear heart**, one idea a child can hold.
3. A **modern school job** that gives the game away (Lost Property, Pond and Puddings, Soup Club).

Skip or soften:

- Anything involving child sacrifice, torture or graphic death. Moloch was declined for this reason.
- Gods from living religions get a warm, respectful portrayal: good and wise, never silly or mocked.
- Sexual or romantic storylines. Stay with friendship, courage, craft, weather, animals and the natural world.
- If a myth has a dark part, choose a gentler episode, or soften it (for example, Thiazi is not killed in the Idun story; he is given a job).

### Living faiths, festivals and many names

- **Festival stories** can cover living beliefs. Story 26 (Lakshmi at Diwali) is the model. For faiths that do not depict God or the Prophet (many Muslim and Jewish families), make a family, a grandparent, food and light the characters, not God. Ask someone from the community to read the story, or use that faith's own children's materials. Check festival dates before writing, as many move with the moon.
- **"Many names, many faces" stories** (one figure who answers to different names, or looks different to different children) are a fair way to extend the series. Frame them as "people tell it their own way", never "all gods are the same", and have each introduce a tradition not yet used.
- **Christianity** is not yet covered. If wanted, it goes in as one tradition among many, told with the same warmth and care as the rest.

## Narration

Stories are narrated by one ElevenLabs voice. The Markdown keeps normal spelling, italics and `## Part` headings, and the narrator (in the Stories repo) cleans these up and applies `narration/pronunciations.json` at generation time. There is no multi-voice pipeline (the Stories narrator dropped an unused one); see the tracker's to-do list.

Write for a parent's voice too. Story 39 was read aloud by a parent (no audio credits) and went down very well: a narration-heavy story with few speakers, short lines and clear "who did what" is easy to perform. Name the twins in every sentence where both are present, and narrate animal sounds rather than spelling them as speech.

## Planning a story with the family

The best details in story 39 came from asking, not inventing: Willow eating her mouse in public ("She's eating the BUTT!"), Alex turning the tap on too hard, Felix's feather toy, Willow sprinting ahead then looking betrayed. Before settling any beat that rests on the real twins or the real Willow, ask the family what really happens, and build from that.

For a planning session: research the real myth or folklore first (web search, original tales over retellings, and say plainly what is in the source and what must be softened or dropped); offer two or three distinct concepts, each with persona notes and a cross-review; recommend one; and wait for a choice before outlining. Say plainly if the user's starting idea would work better elsewhere (story 39's Cat Sìth was moved to the folklore series because its offering repeated story 35's lesson and two magical visitors crowded the god).

## Starting your own version

1. Fork or copy this repo.
2. Rewrite `docs/Story_Bible.md` with your own children, pet, friends and school.
3. Empty `stories/` and `docs/Story_Archive.md`, and keep `docs/God_Tracker.md`'s candidate list.
4. Ask Claude Code to "write the next story" and the `god-story-process` skill takes over.
5. Publishing and narration are handled by the separate Stories app repo (see the README).
